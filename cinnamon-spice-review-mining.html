Executive summary

Across four and a half years and 260 guests, Cinnamon Spice earns something close to a perfect record — not one review below four stars, ever — and the reason is remarkably consistent: couples drive two or three hours from Dallas and Oklahoma City to spend an anniversary inside a spa-cabin with a sauna, a hot tub, a three-head rain shower and what one guest counted as five separate ways to have a fire. That product is real, it is unusual for a one-bedroom, and it conveys with the deed. What does not convey is the thing guests praise most of all — Airbnb's own review tags rank “Hospitality” first at 95 mentions, ahead of location and the hot tub — and underneath that sits the finding that actually moves the underwriting: guests are still delighted, but far fewer of them are coming.

The diagnosis. Rating holding while volume falls is a specific signature: the stay is not failing — demand for it is. Guests who book are as happy as ever; far fewer people are choosing it. Three honest caveats before pricing this in

  1. Reviews proxy bookings, they do not count them. Roughly 60–70% of guests review, and that share can drift. A 61% five-month drop is far outside normal drift — but it is not a T-12 statement.
  2. August 2026 is understated. Guests have up to 14 days to review, so mid-August stays onward have not landed. The August figure of 1 will rise somewhat.
  3. The benign explanation is actually worse. If bookings shifted to VRBO or the seller's own direct site, Airbnb reviews would fall while revenue held — but that means an even larger share of revenue rides on channels that may not convey. Both readings damage the thesis; only a T-12 by channel separates them.

What “4.99× = 20.06%” means, and why we call it a left-tail exception

Both numbers are the same test stated two ways — how many years of gross revenue you are paying, and the same figure inverted as a yield:

TestCalculationResult at ask
Price-to-revenue multiple$485,000 ÷ $97,2854.99× — under our 5× ceiling Passes
Revenue yield on price$97,285 ÷ $485,00020.06% — above our 20% floor Passes
⚠️ It passes by almost nothing. At a 20% floor the revenue needed is $97,000 — so the entire cushion is $285 of annual gross, about one cancelled weekend. On the price side, the maximum that still clears is $486,425, a cushion of $1,425. Both are roughly 0.3%. There is no margin of safety in this number at all.

Why “left-tail exception.” Screening runs at the market level first: take the median price-to-revenue multiple across comparable listings and ask whether a typical purchase there could work. Hochatown's median is ~8.5× — a typical listing costs about 8.5 years of gross — so the market itself failed the screen. But a median is the middle of a distribution, not all of it. The left tail is the cheap end: the few listings priced far below what their market normally charges for a dollar of revenue. At 4.99× against a median of 8.5×, this one sits at roughly 59% of the going rate — an exception to its own market's failure. Across six screened markets it is the only listing to clear the bar at asking price. That is why it survived after Broken Bow was set aside, and why it earned this depth of diligence.

And it frames the risk exactly. With a $285 cushion, the 4.99× depends entirely on $97,285 being both accurate and repeatable. If that figure is a peak rather than a run-rate, the multiple does not drift — it jumps. At an all-channel −42% the property lands at 8.51×, which is the 8.5× median that already failed. The left-tail exception does not degrade gracefully; it collapses back into the market it came from.

So the Zillow-style claim of “gross revenue in the 90s” is a real 2025 figure, not a 2026 projection. It is the seller quoting her best trailing year, which is normal and not misleading — but it is a historical number being used to price a forward asset.

🔎 What could explain April — testing each theory

The signature to explain is specific: bookings fell hard, guest satisfaction did not move at all. That shape rules some theories in and others out before any diligence happens.

The logic that does most of the work here. Anything that degrades the stay shows up as lower ratings. Anything that degrades discoverability, availability or competitiveness shows up as fewer bookings. A prospective guest browsing Airbnb cannot know what is happening next door — so on-site nuisances cannot cause a booking collapse, and demand-side causes cannot cause a rating collapse. Ratings held perfectly. So the cause is almost certainly on the demand side, not at the property.

The listing-pause theory deserves the most weight, and it is the friendliest one. It is the only single explanation that accounts for the full shape — flat Q1, sharp April break, and no recovery after the contract died. It is also entirely recoverable: search ranking rebuilds as bookings resume. If that is what happened, 2026 badly understates what this property earns in normal operation — which would move the supportable price back toward the top of the range, not the bottom.

What would confirm or kill each one

  1. Was the Airbnb listing ever snoozed, paused, or set to unavailable in 2026? If yes, on what dates? — tests the ranking theory, and it is a simple factual question.
  2. Nights blocked by month, with the reason. Separates a closed calendar from genuine absence of demand.
  3. Have any new cabins been built or newly listed within sight or earshot since 2024? The agent will know the street.
  4. Have the subdivision CC&Rs been amended since purchase — any rental cap, licence limit or new restriction? — the recorded plat and covenants answer this without needing the seller.
  5. ADR by month, 2025 versus 2026. Rules the pricing theory in or out immediately.
  6. Was there any construction on adjacent lots in 2025–26? Reviews say no, but confirm at the site visit — and look for cleared lots or foundations, which signal what is coming.
Worth holding onto: your instinct that new houses may have appeared while the reviews stayed excellent is exactly right, and it is the version of this that the review data cannot see. Guests rate the cabin they stayed in — they do not rate how many competitors it now has. A supply-side squeeze is invisible in reviews and fatal to bookings. That is precisely why it has to be checked on the ground and in the records rather than in the guest feedback.
